Challenge 05 · Quantum game

Fly a moth home before the lamps catch it.

Every hedge in this night town was read from the shots of a quantum circuit, and one real shot decides which lamps are lit. The moth's own light reflex will try to keep it circling.

How it was made

What the quantum engine did

Atlas labyrinth-v1 gives every square of the town one qubit. A street between two squares asks for their qubits to agree (ZZ = +1), and a hedge asks for them to disagree (ZZ = −1). The engine starts from |+⟩ on every qubit, applies 3 sweeps of ZZ preparation steps over the grid (each a third of a full rotation; engine defaults), then measures 4096 shots.

Lamp Lane is 4 × 5 = 20 qubits, run on IBM ibm_fez hardware (IBM job db1psfmegvvc73bi0fk0), real noise and all. Its measured hedges match the plan on 24 of 31 edges (77%). Hedge Row is 10 × 12 = 120 qubits, run on IBM ibm_fez hardware (IBM job db1pvqbid5ic73ergopg), real noise and all. Its measured hedges match the plan on 129 of 218 edges (59%). Night Town is 12 × 13 = 156 qubits, run on IBM ibm_fez hardware (IBM job db1j3feegvvc73bhmqc0), real noise and all. Its measured hedges match the plan on 185 of 287 edges (64%). Two earlier runs of the same towns are kept as comparisons, not levels (Figure 4): Lamp Lane on Atlas's noiseless Aer emulator (its ceiling is 20 qubits) matches the plan on 30 of 31 edges (97%) and disagrees with the ibm_fez run on 6 of them; Hedge Row on IBM ibm_miami (IBM job db1jh9ivog1s73fi7ejg) matches the plan on 140 of 218 edges (64%) and disagrees with the ibm_fez run on 105 of them. The mean of plan sign × measured ⟨ZZ⟩ is 0.31 on the emulator, and on hardware it falls to 0.075 (Lamp Lane, ibm_fez), 0.008 (Hedge Row, ibm_fez), 0.013 (Night Town, ibm_fez), 0.018 (Hedge Row, ibm_miami). Noise washes out most of the correlation, yet 59% to 77% of edges still match the plan, where coin flips would match about half. That faint trace is what the noise remembers. On ibm_fez the smallest town kept the most: 0.075 at 20 qubits, against 0.008 at 120 and 0.013 at 156. The engine reports no layout or circuit depth, so we can't say which part of the circuit made the difference.

A town grid needs four neighbours per square. Heron chips such as ibm_fez give each qubit at most three (IBM's heavy-hex layout), so a grid with every neighbour coupled cannot sit on that chip without routing (extra swap gates). Nighthawk (ibm_miami) has 120 qubits on a square lattice with 218 couplers, the same shape as the 10 × 12 town, so the grid could map onto it one-to-one. The engine does not report the layout or circuit depth it used, so we can't confirm that. Hedge Row ran on both chips: the same 10 × 12 town on ibm_fez (the level) and on ibm_miami (a comparison run, Figure 4). On ibm_fez its measured hedges match the plan on 129 of 218 edges (59%, mean 0.008); on ibm_miami, 140 of 218 (64%, mean 0.018). The two runs disagree on 105 of 218 edges, about what two independent samplings with those match rates would give (about 103). Reported QPU time: 3 s on ibm_fez and 19 s on ibm_miami. One run per chip is not a benchmark of either chip.

The streets themselves were planned classically: a seeded random maze plus extra loops (town.py). The quantum state only approximates that plan, so it can close a planned street or open a planned hedge. Turn on Plan vs measured to see every edge where the measured state disagrees with the plan (orange dashes). In the pixel-art Scene the same edges get white dashes.

04

The science

Why do moths circle lamps?

Not because they fly toward the light. Fabian, Sondhi, Allen, Theobald and Lin filmed insects in 3D around artificial lights (Nature Communications 15, 689, 2024; doi:10.1038/s41467-024-44785-3). They found that the insects keep their back, the dorsal side, turned toward the brightest light. Under an open sky that reflex keeps them level. Next to a lamp it makes them fly across the light instead of at it, and they get stuck circling, stalling or flipping over.

The moth here follows a flat, top-down cartoon of that reflex. The brightest lamp it can see (hedges block the view) swings its heading until the lamp sits just ahead of its side. Left alone in open space at pull 1, it settles into an orbit about 0.44 squares from the bulb, one loop every 1.5 s or so, and burns energy in the glare. Among hedges and neighbouring lamps it swings back and forth instead, still held in the light. You steer against it. The pull slider scales the reflex from 0 (off) to 2×. Higher pull means tighter, faster orbits.

05

What this does not claim

The flight model is classical and simplified: a flat steering rule inspired by the dorsal-light-response finding. It is not a simulation of insect flight, and it is not what Fabian et al. modelled in 3D. The quantum engine knows nothing about moths. It turns a street plan into correlations and samples them, and the game reads those samples as hedges and lamps. Every level ran on IBM ibm_fez hardware. The emulator run in Figure 4 is a comparison, not a level, and ran on a noiseless classical simulator of the circuit. For the hardware runs the engine reports the backend and IBM job ID but not the transpiled layout or depth, and one run per chip is not a benchmark of either chip. Nothing here shows quantum advantage, and the lamp patterns are not certified randomness. Start, home, lamp positions within each square, and the rule that the start and home squares have no lamp are game design choices.
