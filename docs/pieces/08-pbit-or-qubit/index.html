How to play

You are in control

  1. Start the showdown, and turn the J dial or pick a graph between envelopes (the boss picks its own).

  3. Call it: Sauna (p-bits, key S) or Fridge (qubits, key F) before the clock runs out. Streaks multiply your score.

How it was made

What ran where
What graph-v1 did on 20 qubits

graph-v1 can't take an energy function. It takes targets: a bloch target for each qubit and a relationship target (a two-qubit Pauli expectation) for each coupled pair. For every problem we sent the same recipe:

  • 20 bloch targets X = 1: every qubit starts in |+⟩, so no bit is favoured (zero field).
  • One relationship target per edge: ZZ = ⟨sisj⟩, the exact Boltzmann edge correlation for that J (enumerated over all 220 states), with rotation fraction = (2/π)·asin|ZZ|. On a lone edge, the engine's partial rotation from |++⟩ gives ZZ = sin(π·fraction/2), so it lands on the target.
  • num_qubits = 20, the engine's maximum (one qubit per node), and shots = 20. graph-v1 returns only its 20 most frequent bitstrings, so with 20 shots that list holds every shot and nothing is cut. Each envelope shows 16 of those 20 shots, so envelopes from the same job overlap.

The same parameters ran on Atlas's emulator (Aer, a classical simulation of the circuit, noiseless) and were sent, with mode: "qpu", to IBM's ibm_fez. Every job ID is in the readout and in PARAMS.md.

graph-v1 also returns its own tomography of the prepared state. Tick "show graph-v1's own reported tomography" to compare it with the shots: on several problems it disagrees with them (for example, on the cold ring it reads about 0 on 8 of the 20 edges, where the shots average 0.71). We read this as the engine tracking its state approximately, which we infer and haven't verified, so the charts trust the shots.

The tick box sits under the edge-correlation chart in The data; every job is listed in Jobs and credits.

What THRML did

For each problem, IsingEBM with the same edges and couplings, zero biases and β = 1, blocks from a graph colouring (2 colours for the ring and ladder, 3 for the spin glass), and 64 independent chains: 400 warm-up sweeps, then 64 samples each, 10 sweeps apart. That makes 4,096 samples per problem. Every game batch takes 16 samples from 16 different chains. So does every sauna envelope in the arcade.

Checked against exact enumeration of all 220 states: the largest edge-correlation error is shown in the readout (at most 0.04). THRML ran on a laptop CPU through JAX, not on Extropic hardware. It did not run on an MTJ chip either.

The web app behind this page

This artifact can't call any API, so it replays the real batches listed above. The entry also ships a small web app: a Node server with /api/graph, which calls Atlas server-side with the key from .env (the key never reaches the browser), plus this same front end with a live panel. Run it with node server.js inside app/. It is set up for Vercel (vercel.json, api/graph.js) but has not been deployed.

The science

Thermal noise against quantum noise

Why p-bits against qubits?

A p-bit is a bit that keeps flipping. Extropic is building chips in which each p-bit turns on with probability σ(2·field), where the field comes from its neighbours (the p-bit idea is from Camsari, Sutton and Datta, Applied Physics Reviews, 2019). Update the nodes colour by colour and you get block Gibbs sampling: the chip settles into the Boltzmann distribution p(s) ∝ exp(Σ Jij sisj). Its randomness is thermal. THRML is Extropic's open-source JAX library that simulates exactly these programs.

A qubit is set deterministically by gates. Its randomness only appears when you measure it (the Born rule), and on real hardware decoherence, gate errors and readout errors add more. Both kinds of machine end up handing you bitstrings, so the question is whether the bitstrings remember which physics made them.

A note on words. Warm and cold on the boards and charts describe the coupling J, as in a statistical-physics model: J 0.4 is warm and loose, J 1.0 is cold and ordered. The sauna and the fridge are the machines: room-temperature heat for a hardware p-bit such as the magnetic tunnel junction drawn above, and a dilution refrigerator at about 10–20 mK for superconducting qubits.

Anatomy of the two fighters

What this does not claim

Honesty

No advantage is claimed in either direction. Emulated qubits are a classical simulation of the circuit. Every qubit statistic comes from 20 shots and carries the error bar shown. The p-bits are THRML software running on a CPU. The qubits did not solve the Ising problem: we told them the answer's pair correlations and asked them to build a state that has them. Nor are the p-bits a magnetic tunnel junction: the junction in the drawings stands for hardware p-bits. On most problems that makes the two sources hard to tell apart by construction, and the chance meter will say so. The drawings are illustrations of the kinds of hardware involved, and their temperatures are approximate general figures.

What is real data in the game, and what is decoration
  • Every frame of the magnet board and every column of the tape is one real sample: a THRML p-bit sample, or one graph-v1 shot from the Atlas emulator or ibm_fez. Up = spin +1 = bit 0; down = spin −1 = bit 1. A dashed bond is broken: its two magnets disagree when the coupling wants them to agree (or the reverse on a spin glass's negative bonds).
  • The free layer's arrow and the resistance trace under the junction replay spin 0 of THRML chain 0, sample by sample (10 sweeps apart). They are software samples drawn the way an MTJ would show them, not readings from a junction.
  • The chance meter is an exact one-sided binomial test of your calls against a fair coin. Time-outs are not calls and are left out.
  • Plates 1 to 4 use the Atlas emulator's shots (a classical simulation of the circuit, no fridge involved). The boss uses the two real ibm_fez batches. The fridge drawing is the level map, not a picture of the machine that ran the emulator.
  • Every tile on the classic board is one real sample too, and the charts in The data are computed from the same samples and shots.

Steam, frost, screen shake, callouts, the sounds and the pulsing level marker are decoration. The temperatures on the drawings are approximate general figures for these machines, not readings from our jobs.
