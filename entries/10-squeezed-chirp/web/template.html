      How the game scores you

      The target is the chirp's loudest moment in the original (unblurred) real spectrogram. Its time is the power-weighted centre of the moments whose total power (20 to 340 Hz) is within 5 % of the loudest, at – ms on our clock (0 ms = GPS 1126259462.422, close to the published merger time); the original audio is loudest within 2 ms of it. Its pitch is the power-weighted centre frequency of the strongest cells at those moments, – Hz. The audio and the screen you work from are the blurred output for your setting, which shifts and spreads the peak a little: that is the game.

      Timing = 1000 × (original time-width ÷ this output's time-width)² × e−½(miss ÷ tolerance)², with the tolerance = 2 × this output's measured time-width (18 to 20 ms of real time, 144 to 164 ms in the slowed audio). Pitch is the same with the frequency widths (tolerance 24 to 26 Hz). A catch inside one tolerance raises the candidate alert. Each round starts with a 3, 2, 1 count, so you know when the chirp begins; the screen only shows what has already played.

      Because the squeezer moves blur from one axis to the other, the time-sharp settings cap pitch and the pitch-sharp settings cap timing: . The highest possible total is at r = 1, where the measured width product is smallest. The blur this setting adds, √(width² − original width²), is the squeeze ellipse: its area stays within a few per cent of 14.5 ms·Hz across all five ratios.

      These are game rules made from real measurements, not a detection statistic. LIGO's real low-latency searches found GW150914 without any human pressing a button.

      How it was made

      open a drawer
      What the quantum engine did

      Atlas blur-core-v1 amplitude-encodes the 257 × 257 spectrogram grid into a statevector on a Gray-coded register, so neighbouring grid points differ by one bit. Each axis is padded to 512, which makes 9 + 9 = 18 qubits; the engine reports "Recovered 18-qubit grid from measurement" for every job. An Rx rotation is applied to each qubit, with one strength for the 9 time qubits and another for the 9 frequency qubits, at reach = 0 (local). The exact output probabilities (no shots) are read back and rescaled to the input's maximum.

      Six jobs: five ratios on s_time × s_freq = 0.25 (r = 1/4, 1/2, 1, 2, 4) and an isotropic control with a single scalar strength 0.5, which matched r = 1 to the last digit. The blocky stripes along the more strongly blurred axis are the Gray-coded register showing through, not anything in the gravitational wave.

      Because the control matched r = 1 exactly, the ISO position on the squeezer dial plays r = 1's audio and grid; its own job ID and widths are listed in Jobs and credits.

      The science

      open a drawer
      How LIGO really squeezes light

      A laser's quantum noise has two parts, or quadratures, whose uncertainties trade against each other. In a gravitational-wave detector, phase-quadrature noise is shot noise and dominates at high frequency; amplitude-quadrature noise pushes the mirrors around (radiation pressure) and dominates at low frequency.

      Squeezed light makes one quadrature quieter by making the other louder. An optical parametric oscillator (a PPKTP crystal pumped with 532 nm light) makes squeezed vacuum, which enters the detector through the output port by way of the output Faraday isolator. LIGO first ran with squeezed light through a whole observing run in O3 (2019), gaining up to 3 dB above 50 Hz (Tse et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 123, 231107, 2019). GW150914 was recorded in 2015, before that. That is why the squeezer in the game is labelled “what-if”.

      For O4 each detector gained a 300 m filter cavity that rotates the squeezing with frequency, so high frequencies get quieter shot noise and low frequencies quieter radiation pressure. Ganapathy et al. (Phys. Rev. X 13, 041021, 2023) report detector noise cut by up to 4.0 dB at Hanford and 5.8 dB at Livingston, a 15 to 18 % longer range, and up to 65 % more detections expected.

      Here the "squeeze" is only an analogy: two blur strengths with a fixed product, one per axis of a spectrogram. The game's lesson (you cannot sharpen both axes at once; put the blur where it hurts you least) mirrors the logic of frequency-dependent squeezing, but nothing here simulates light.

      The night of 14 September 2015

      At 09:50:45 UTC (04:50 in Livingston, 02:50 in Hanford) both LIGO detectors recorded GW150914. It reached Livingston first and Hanford 6.9 ms later. A low-latency search for generic transients reported it within three minutes. The detectors were still in engineering mode: the first observing run, O1, formally began four days later, on 18 September.

      Published estimates (Abbott et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 116, 061102, 2016): black holes of 36 and 29 solar masses merged into one of 62; about 3.0 solar masses (3.0 M☉c²) left as gravitational waves, with a peak power of about 3.6 × 1056 erg/s (200 M☉c² per second); luminosity distance about 410 Mpc (redshift 0.09, roughly 1.3 billion light years); peak strain 1.0 × 10−21; the signal swept from 35 to 250 Hz; network SNR 24; false-alarm rate below 1 per 203 000 years (more than 5.1σ). The later GWTC-1 catalogue values on GWOSC are 35.6 and 30.6 merging to 63.1 solar masses, 3.1 M☉c² radiated, at about 440 Mpc.

      The control-room clock in the game is GPS time converted to UTC (GPS − 17 s in 2015) and then to Central Daylight Time. It runs 8× slow, with the audio.

      The real detector, part by part

      Each LIGO site is an L of two 4 km arms at right angles. Livingston has a corner station (laser, beam splitter, input optics, control room) and an end station at the end of each arm. The beams travel in 1.2 m stainless-steel tubes held below 1 µPa, under arched concrete covers, through a working loblolly-pine forest. The steel tube wall is only about 3 mm thick, ringed with stiffeners about every metre, and the slab under it was levelled so precisely that the beam stays centred although the Earth curves away by nearly a metre over 4 km. At Livingston the X arm runs on a bearing of 252.3° and the Y arm on 162.3° (GWOSC detector constants).

      In O1 a 20 W Nd:YAG laser at 1064 nm passed an input mode cleaner and a power-recycling mirror, which built it to 700 W on the beam splitter; each arm is a Fabry-Perot cavity between an input and an end test mass that stored about 100 kW. The test masses are 40 kg fused-silica mirrors, 34 cm across, each the last stage of a quadruple pendulum on an active seismic-isolation platform. A signal-recycling mirror shapes the response, and the gravitational-wave signal leaves through the dark port, past the output Faraday isolator and the output mode cleaner, to photodetectors.

      The three plates are original drawings made from these published facts, not traced from photographs. Plate I is projected in the page from the true geometry (two 4 km arms at 90°, seen from about 1.7 km beyond the corner); the covers are drawn several times too wide so they show, the buildings are simplified, and the logged stands are placed for illustration. Plate II is a plan with the arms broken and the recycling folds (PR2, PR3, SR2, SR3) drawn straight. Plate III is a side view of the quadruple pendulum, not to scale. Tap any number to find its part.

      What this does not claim

      honesty notes

      This is an analogy to squeezing, not squeezed light. The quantum circuit ran on Atlas's classical statevector simulator, not on quantum hardware. The fixed product is a product of rotation strengths: the measured width product is lowest at r = 1 (114.6 ms·Hz) and rises at both ends, unlike an ideal squeezed state. A classical Gaussian blur shows the same qualitative trade, so nothing here is a quantum advantage, and nothing improves gravitational-wave detection. Whitening, filtering, the spectrogram, the width measurements and the audio are all classical. The game's scores are classical too.

      The game is fiction around real data: you play an imagined operator, and real alerts come from software. The source view is an illustration: point-lens bending of a made-up star field around shadows sized by the published masses, with the orbit following a Newtonian chirp f ∝ (tc − t)−3/8 fitted to the measured ridge between −89 and −21 ms; sizes and separations are not to scale, there is no accretion disc, and the ring-down is a drawn relaxation. The end test masses in the live strip and on Plates II and III, and the ring of test particles, move with the real ridge power of your setting at each moment, times the phase of the fitted chirp: the size of the motion is enlarged about 1015 times, and the swing is drawn, not measured.
