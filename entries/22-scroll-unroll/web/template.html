Why a rolled scroll?

Some scrolls can never be opened: the En-Gedi scroll was burnt to a lump, and the Herculaneum papyri were carbonised by Vesuvius in 79 CE. X-ray CT can see inside, and “virtual unwrapping” reads them without touching them: find the sheet in the scan, sample the scan along it, and lay it flat (Seales et al., Science Advances 2016).

En-Gedi was readable because its ink absorbs X-rays more strongly than the parchment. Herculaneum's carbon ink barely shows in CT; Parker et al. (PLoS ONE 2019) showed it still leaves a subtle signature, and machine learning has since been trained to find it.

This piece uses none of that data. The scroll is generated from scratch: an Archimedean spiral of papyrus with a gentle wobble, fibres, cracks, CT ring artefacts, grain, carbon flecks and abraded strokes, and one line of Greek written into one inner stretch of the sheet.

What the quantum engine did

Atlas blur-v1 took the ink layer alone (a 1024 px image, white ink on black) and a mask disk around the roll. The mask's 1001 × 1001 px bounding box needs ⌈log21001⌉ + ⌈log21001⌉ = 10 + 10 = 20 qubits, the engine's maximum. This count comes from the engine's documented rule; the engine does not report one.

The engine encodes each pixel's brightness into a quantum state (the QuantumBlur library maps pixel coordinates to qubits with a Gray code), applies one rotation gate per qubit (rx here) with an angle set by strength, and reads the state back into pixels. Reach sets how far a pixel can travel: at reach 0 the letters break into little Gray-code blocks but stay put; at reach 0.5 or 1 the ink is thrown across the whole roll as a plaid of echoes.

The damage slider steps through the nine completed jobs, sorted by how much ink brightness lands off the true ink. “Line kept” is the Pearson correlation between the blurred and clean ink over the hidden line, measured on the flattened sheet. Every scrambled ink layer is a downloaded engine output, added to the papyrus in your browser; “Ink layer only” shows it untouched. The job ID updates as you go.

What is classical here

Everything except the blur: generating the scroll, compositing the ink onto the papyrus (scan = papyrus + 0.2 × ink), the contrast window, the tint, the unrolling animation, and the virtual unwrapping. The unwrap samples the scan along the spiral we generated, so it knows the sheet exactly. A real pipeline must first find the sheet in a noisy 3D volume, which is the hard part.

The scan is printed in the page's ink: denser material is a deeper blue and empty air is paper. That colouring is a classical display choice too. “Ink layer only” is the exception: it shows the engine output in its own greyscale.

The desk above the CT slice is a classical pixel-art illustration of the same state. Places along the sheet are to scale; the roll is drawn larger than it would be at that scale, and its spiral turns with the real one. The moth stands where the reading window is and reacts to the real numbers: it reads only where the job's measured “line kept” passes the same threshold the page uses to count the line as read, and the letters it uncovers are the stretches you have actually read. The ash is one fleck per 2 % of ink the current job moved off the true ink. Nothing on the desk is a new measurement.

What this does not claim

No real scroll was scanned and no real scroll data was used. The blur is a stand-in for damage, not a physical model of fire or carbonisation. blur-v1 ran on Atlas's classical statevector simulator; nothing ran on quantum hardware. Nothing is recovered by the quantum step: it only destroys. This is not the Vesuvius Challenge method and says nothing about how well that method works.

One artistic liberty: in a real scroll a line of text runs across many CT slices. Here the letters are written into the thickness of the layer within one slice, so a single 1024 px image can carry a readable line.
