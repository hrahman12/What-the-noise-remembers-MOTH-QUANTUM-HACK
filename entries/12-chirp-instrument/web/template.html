Challenge 02 · Make it audible

Play the last fifth of a second of two black holes.

Real LIGO strain from GW150914, tracked half-wave by half-wave into MIDI. A quantum blur on a simulated 20-qubit register then scatters it. Tap through it, scrub the blur, loop the merger, and jam over it.

  2. Scrub the quantum blur's reach and hear the merger smear into its neighbours. Its cloud of real notes spreads across the take.
  3. Loop any stretch by dragging across the roll, then jam on the keys over it. The tape under the scene loops the same way.

How it was made

classical tracking · one quantum step

Where the notes come from

GW150914 (measured track). We whiten 32 s of real H1 and L1 strain and build a wavelet scalogram (the glow behind the notes). Then we follow its ridge with a path that may only rise in frequency. The ridge phase is cut into half-cycles: one note per half-wave of the real signal. Each note's pitch is the ridge frequency ×4 (two octaves up, so 30–175 Hz lands in a piano range). Its loudness is that half-wave's peak whitened strain, separately for each detector. Time is stretched ×100: 0.22 s becomes 22 s.

Livingston recorded the wave 7.3 ms before Hanford (we measured this by cross-correlation; the sign flips because the two detectors' arms are rotated). Stretched ×100, that becomes a 0.73 s canon between the left and right voices. The rising pitch and quickening rhythm are the chirp itself. A leading-order fit to the ridge gives a chirp mass near 35 M☉ (detector frame). It's a crude check: the catalogue value is 27.9 M☉ in the source frame, about 31 M☉ in the detector frame.

GW170817 (model-guided track). The neutron-star signal is too faint to follow cycle by cycle, so its pitch line uses the leading-order chirp law with GWOSC's catalogue chirp mass (1.186 M☉, z = 0.01). Only the merger time is fitted to the data. There is one note per 16 wave cycles, and each note's loudness is the real detector power along that stretch of the track. Along the track the power averages 6.8× the off-track level; the same test with the merger time shifted by ±2 s gives 1.65×. L1's well-known glitch 1.1 s before the merger is gated out.

The scene. What it draws from the data: one ripple per real half-wave (or per 16-cycle note for GW170817), its ink from the measured strain, its arrival at each detector exactly when that voice's note sounds, which arm stretches (it flips every half-wave), the orbit turning a quarter turn per half-wave, the separation from the ridge frequency (r ∝ f−2/3) and the Newtonian km and v/c readouts from it, the merger at the measured peak, the strain traces between the observatories, every dot in the blur cloud, and one grain per blurred note that starts. What is real-world geometry: the shadows' 36 : 29 : 62 sizes; each observatory's arms at their true bearings, 3994.5 m long, with the end stations at 4 km (and Hanford's mid stations at 2 km), projected through a camera over the site; and at Hanford the two named summits of the Rattlesnake Hills at their real bearings and heights. What is drawn: the lensing strength, the stars, band and galaxy, the ringdown shape (its 251 Hz, 4 ms timing is GR's prediction), the neutron stars' fields and beams, the flash, the grains' paths, the buildings' footprints and their enlargement (the corner stations 1.6×, the mid and end stations 3.5×), the cut-away optics (a schematic), the light in the tubes, the 2-pixel stretch, the hills' height (×2.5), the ground and forest textures, and every distance between the source and the Earth. The ripple speed is chosen so that the 0.73 s canon fits between the two observatories on your screen.

What the quantum engine did

Atlas blur-midi-v1 (Quantum Blur for MIDI) turns each MIDI track into a piano roll (pitch × time) and blurs it as a height map with a quantum circuit on Atlas's simulator. It then rebuilds notes wherever the blurred velocity rises above a noise gate (threshold 0.1 of the loudest). In the engine's own words, reach 0 is "fully local: only neighbouring pitches/times mix" and reach 1 is "fully non-local: any pitch can mix with any other". The engine describes the blur as unitary and information-preserving; the noise gate that turns it back into notes is not.

We asked for qubits = 20, the engine's maximum per pass, and a step of 2 MIDI ticks. Quantum Blur stores a w × h grid in ⌈log₂ w⌉ + ⌈log₂ h⌉ qubits (the rule Atlas documents for blur-v1). Each of our tracks is about 42 pitch rows (the 32-semitone span plus the engine's 15% margin) × 11,600 steps, so that is 6 + 14 = 20 qubits. This is our calculation; the engine doesn't report the width. The archived job records show qubits = 20 and resolution = 2 were sent, and the reach-0.5 output spans exactly MIDI 41–82, the 42 rows we assumed.

What you hear at reach 0: every half-wave becomes a two-note cluster with a neighbouring semitone, plus faint 2–6 ms pre-echoes just before each onset. At reach 0.5 the merger's notes scatter across the whole 25 s, before and after the event, as a cloud of 9,444 mostly tiny notes. Notes shorter than 40 ms are too short to hear, so the synth plays them as 40 ms grains, capped at 96 voices at once.

The science

Who is who in the scene · data or decoration

Each card is one thing in the scene: what it is in the real event, and which parts of its drawing come from the data.

The black holes of GW150914, drawn as what you would see: each dark disc is a shadow, √27 GM/c² in radius, so the two are sized by mass, 36 : 29 M☉ (Abbott et al., PRL 116, 061102). A thin photon ring edges each one, and the background sky is bent around them: the page ray-traces two point-mass lenses for every pixel, every frame (the lensing strength is chosen for the eye). They turn a quarter orbit per real half-wave and close in as r ∝ f−2/3; the km and fraction-of-c readouts are Newtonian Kepler estimates from the ridge frequency. The orbit is drawn 1.4× wider than the shadows' own scale, so the two stay apart until the last few half-waves, when they flow into one outline.

After the peak, one 62 M☉ hole, its shadow on the same scale. Its outline rings at the remnant's l=m=2 quasi-normal mode, 251 Hz with a 4.0 ms decay (Abbott et al., PRL 116, 221101), slowed ×100 like the music: GR's prediction for the measured remnant, drawn, not measured here. The missing 3 M☉ left as the waves you are hearing.

GW170817's neutron stars, 1.46 and 1.27 M☉ (GWTC-1). Each is about 24 km across (a neutron star's radius barely depends on its mass), so they are drawn the same size: small bright spheres, darkening to the limb, three times the orbit's own scale so that they show, in front of their host galaxy NGC 4993, 40 Mpc away. They turn a quarter orbit per note (really 8 orbits per note) and close in as r ∝ f−2/3 along the model frequency. The flash is decoration: gamma rays arrived 1.7 s after the merger and the kilonova was seen about 11 hours later.

LIGO Hanford, H1, from the air: the camera hangs behind the outside corner of the L and looks along the bisector of the two arms, which run 4 km NW (324°) and SW (234°) across the sagebrush steppe. Each arm is a 1.2 m steel beam tube under an arched concrete cover, with a mid station at 2 km (it held an end mirror of Hanford's former second, 2 km interferometer) and an end station at 4 km. On the skyline are the Rattlesnake Hills, with Rattlesnake Mountain (1,076 m) 17.6 km away at its real bearing; the arrow on the ground points north. The corner station's roof is cut away. Inside, the laser feeds the beam splitter, which sends the light through the two input test masses, down the arms to the end test masses (40 kg fused-silica mirrors, 34 cm across) and back; any difference in arm length leaks light to the photodetector at the dark port. Drawn, not measured: the building footprints (approximate), the corner station at 1.6× and the outer stations at 3.5× their size, the optics' layout (a schematic), and the hills' height (×2.5).

LIGO Livingston, L1: the same instrument, seen the same way along its own arms, which run WSW (252.3°) and SSE (162.3°) through cleared strips in loblolly pine forest. Its buildings stand at the corner and the two ends only: it never had Hanford's 2 km interferometer, so it has no mid stations. When a real half-wave lands, light runs through the corner station and out along both arms, one arm stretches while the other squeezes (its end station shifts 2 px), and which one flips every half-wave because the strain changes sign; the photodetector flashes. At GW150914's peak (strain 10−21) the real change was about 4×10−18 m on a 4 km arm, and the light is infrared and hidden inside the tubes, so the stretch and the light are both drawn. Between the two sites run their real whitened strain traces. Tap a site to mute its voice: its laser goes off.

A ripple is one real half-wave leaving the pair. Its ink is the measured strain: on the sky the lighter, and on the ground the darker, the louder. It lands on Livingston 7.3 ms (0.73 s here) before Hanford, as it did at 09:50:45 UTC on 14 September 2015, when it was night at both sites.

The quantum blur (blur-midi-v1, Atlas simulator, 20 qubits) is not a character: it is a cloud, and every dot in it is a real note of the current setting within 1.5 s of the playhead (across: time; up: pitch). At reach 0 the half-waves become tight clusters; at reach 0.5 the merger's notes scatter across the whole take. One grain flies per blurred note that starts (a real count; the flight and the one-pixel shimmer are decoration). Tap the cloud to change the reach.

What this does not claim

honesty notes

This is a classical sonification of public LIGO data. The tracking, the MIDI, the synth and the WAV renders are all classical. Only the blur is a quantum circuit, and it ran on Atlas's simulator, not on quantum hardware. Nothing here detects, measures or improves anything about gravitational waves, and the blur has no physical link to them: it is an audio effect. There is no quantum advantage. GW170817's pitch comes from a textbook model, not from tracking. The ×100 time stretch and ×4 pitch shift are artistic choices, so this is not what the wave "sounds like". For the real thing, press Hear the detector.

The scene is classical too, and it is a labelled plate, not a simulation: the lensing is a thin-lens sketch through two point masses (a real binary's shadow is more intricate), the separations are Newtonian estimates, the observatories are drawn from published geometry but with their buildings enlarged and the optics as a schematic, and the source, the Earth and the detectors are not drawn to one scale.
