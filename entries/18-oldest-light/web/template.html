The story

Seeds of galaxies

Five short stops. Each "Show me" button sets the sky above to match; nothing moves until you press it.

01

The oldest light

For its first 380,000 years the universe was a glowing fog of plasma. When it cooled to about 3,000 K, atoms formed and light flew free. That light has been travelling ever since, stretched by cosmic expansion into microwaves at 2.725 K. This whole-sky map is centred on the patch you are exploring, outlined in white.

02

Tiny ripples

Typical ripples are about 75 millionths of a degree, a few parts in 100,000 of the 2.725 K average; even the hottest and coldest spots here are only about one part in 10,000. Hover or tap the sky to read the temperature at any point.

03

Quantum seeds

In the standard model of cosmology, these ripples began as quantum fluctuations in the first split second, stretched to astronomical size by a burst of expansion called inflation. Inflation fits the measured pattern well, but nobody has observed it directly. The big cold region in the middle is the so-called Cold Spot, and whether it is unusual is still debated.

04

Gravity takes over

Slightly denser regions pulled in more matter. Over billions of years that grew into the cosmic web of galaxy clusters and galaxies. Each spot here is the seed of a structure hundreds of millions of light-years across; the seeds of single galaxies are far too small to see in this light. On the largest scales, denser regions actually look slightly colder, because light loses energy climbing out of their gravity.

05

Why sharp eyes matter

Every telescope blurs the sky by the size of its beam. COBE (1992) first saw the ripples with a 7° beam. WMAP sharpened that to 0.2–0.9°, and Planck to about 5 arcminutes. Blur the sky and the fine ripples vanish first, and with them the pattern that reveals the universe's age and contents.

How it was made

Engine, qubits, limits
What the quantum engine did

Atlas blur-core-v1 takes a grid of non-negative numbers, amplitude-encodes it onto a Gray-coded qubit register, rotates the qubits with Rx gates and reads the probabilities back out. Neighbouring grid points differ by one bit in a Gray code, so a rotation spills a value's weight onto its neighbours: a blur. "Strength" sets the rotation angle; "reach 0" keeps the spread local.

The sky grid is 257 × 257 temperatures. The engine pads each side to the next power of two, 512, so the register holds 512 × 512 = 218 amplitudes: 9 + 9 = 18 qubits, and the engine reports it ("Recovered 18-qubit grid from measurement"). The sky fills 66,049 of those 262,144 amplitudes (25%); the rest is zero padding.

The circuit ran on Atlas's classical statevector simulator with exact probabilities (no shots). blur-core-v1 has no hardware mode. Every blurred image on this page is a downloaded engine output; the job ID updates as you slide.

Why 18 qubits, not 24

The engine allows up to 24 qubits. We stepped down one notch at a time and measured each limit. Probes marked "free" sent an invalid parameter on purpose, so the API answered without creating a job.

The 20-qubit failures were measured by two sibling pieces in this project (jobs 43503eaa and 02b057fb on a dense 640 × 640 grid; a8a739fb on a 528 × 528 grid that was 99.6% zeros). All ran and then hit the same platform limit on the size of the returned result.

How the quantum blur differs from a real beam

A telescope beam averages neighbouring sky, so contrast drops as it blurs. The quantum blur is unitary: it shuffles weight between Gray-code neighbours, so contrast stays high (about 95% even at full strength) while the spot pattern is scrambled into square blocks, the shape of the qubit register showing through. Switch to "Gaussian beam" to see a classical blur of the same width.

The "equivalent beam" is a classical least-squares fit: the Gaussian smoothing of the original that comes closest to each engine output, added in quadrature to the map's own 1° resolution. "Spot pattern kept" is the correlation between the original and the blurred sky after both are band-passed to spot scales (11′ to 45′). "Contrast kept" is the ratio of their standard deviations. Both are classical measurements of the engine output.

The numbers behind these measurements are in Fig. 2 under The data; the story of the ripples is in the cards above.

What this does not claim

Honesty labels

The quantum blur is an analogy for a telescope's resolution. It is not a physical model of inflation, of the early universe, or of any real telescope. Nothing quantum about the cosmos was simulated: the quantum circuit only blurs a grid of numbers, and it ran on a classical simulator, not quantum hardware. There is no quantum advantage here.

Classical steps, all labelled: reading and projecting the WMAP map, mapping temperatures to integers 0–999 for the engine (it accepts only non-negative numbers), removing the average shift the engine's rescaling adds (+1 to +32 µK), the colour map, the Gaussian and COBE-like comparisons, and all fits. The COBE view is an idealised, noise-free smoothing, not COBE data.

The ILC map is a foreground-cleaned combination meant for display. The WMAP team cautions that its structure below about 10° is less certain, so it is not used here for science, only to show the sky. The account of quantum origins is the standard inflationary picture, well supported by the data but not directly observed.

The cartoon, honestly: what the sprites show

Mapper is a cartoon in the style of NASA's WMAP probe (two back-to-back dishes above a flat sun shield). It is not to scale, has a face, and does not fly like the real one, which spun and swept the whole sky from a point 1.5 million km beyond the Earth. Its blinking, bobbing, squinting (when its eye is very blurred), shivering (when you mark the Cold Spot) and cheering are reactions, not measurements.

The beam circle is drawn at a real size: the map's own 1° on the original side, and on the blurred side the classical best-fit beam of the engine output you chose (or 7° for COBE). The eye on the beam line sits at that beam size, and its fuzziness follows "Spot pattern kept": sharp at 100%, a smudge at COBE's 11%. The mission icons sit at the published beam sizes of Planck (about 5′), WMAP (0.2–0.9°) and COBE (7°).

Photons leave the point you are looking at. Each is orange if the map shown there is warmer than average at that point and lilac if colder; that is all the data they carry. Their wiggle and flight are decoration: real CMB photons are microwaves about 2 mm long.

Galaxy seeds are planted on every local hot spot in the original map hotter than +150 µK (twice the patch's typical ripple), at least 1.1° apart. A blurred map's hot spots are found by the same rule against its own typical ripple, so a blur that only dims the sky does not count as losing spots (that is "Contrast kept"). On the blurred side a seed stays a galaxy if the blurred map still has a hot spot within 0.75°; otherwise it fades. A phantom is a hot spot in the blurred map with no hot spot within 0.75° in the original: the quantum blur's square blocks make many of them, a smooth classical beam almost none. All of this is counted classically, in your browser, on the downloaded engine outputs. Drawing a galaxy on a hot spot is a cartoon: every ripple, hot or cold, seeded structure, and how a spot's temperature relates to what grew there is subtler (card 04).
