    Who's who in the pixel lab

    Tweezy is an atom held in an optical-tweezer beam: the hourglass of light narrows to a focus, and the atom sits in it. Its face at each station is set by that hop's survival F against chance (rule under the lab path), never by hand.

    In the scenes, a small hourglass is an empty tweezer; a round face in it is a loaded tweezer; a glowing ring is an atom the 10:00 pattern excited; a pale ghost is a site the camera called loaded although it was empty; a faded atom is one the camera missed. Every one of them is placed from the cached job data on this page (the comet bitstrings, the decoded photon counts, the planned moves, the measured patterns, the logical success rates, the score's notes, the echo maps, the circuit's <Z> values).

    Motion is decoration on top of that: atoms falling into place, coins flipping, a pan sizzling, reels turning and sound waves rolling out are there to show what a step does, not to measure it. Where a scene replays something the engine did not record (the order the coin tosses landed, the order atoms dropped), its note says so. All sprites are drawn for this piece in the shared ink palette; orange appears only for light, glow and fire.

    The science

    tweezers, Rydberg blockade, and the survival score
    Why a neutral-atom day?

    Neutral-atom quantum computers hold single atoms in optical tweezers. Each run starts the same way: tweezers load at random, roughly half full; a camera images the array; moving tweezers drag spare atoms into the holes of a target zone; then lasers drive atoms to Rydberg states, where atoms closer than the blockade radius cannot both be excited. That rule turns an atom layout into a unit-disk graph (Ebadi et al., Science 376, 2022).

    The stages here follow that routine, but every quantum step is an Atlas engine running gate-model circuits on IBM hardware or on simulators. No atoms were involved. Each stage label says what stood in for what.

    How survival is scored

    Every hop compares what a stage received with what it handed on, on outcomes both sides share: tweezer sites, lanes, graph edges, frame transitions, beats, sound bands. Both are read as probability distributions and scored with the classical (Bhattacharyya) fidelity F = (Σ √(p·q))². F = 1 means nothing was lost.

    For many independent yes/no outcomes (lanes, edges, logical qubits) F is averaged per outcome. Because F is generous to flat patterns, each hop also shows a chance line: the same F after shuffling which outcome each value belongs to (or 0.5 for a coin-flip per outcome). The hops measure different things, so they are not multiplied into one end-to-end number.

    What this does not claim

    Atlas runs gate-model circuits and simulators, not atoms. Some stages ran on real IBM hardware (named on their cards); the rest ran on Atlas simulators or noise-model emulators, and each card says which. No failed job is counted. Mapping choices (which qubit stands for which tweezer, how glass angle or loudness is assigned, the move rules, the threshold) are classical decisions made by this piece, and the cards say so. The Bell witness is a fidelity check on the chip, not certified randomness. Nothing here claims quantum advantage or simulates atom physics.
