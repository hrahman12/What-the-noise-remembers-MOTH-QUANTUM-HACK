Challenge 03 · Three dimensions

Try to hide a moth’s eye from the lamp.

Real moth eyes kill glare with nanopillars. Shade a 3D nanopillar eye with a quantum thin-film shader, stack up to six layers, and see whether the shine dies. In the scene the eye sits under a loupe and a bat watches from wherever you look: does it still spot the shine?

How to play

  1. Orbit the eye: drag it, or focus it and use the arrow keys. It sits in the loupe, and the bat flies to where you look from.
  2. Drag the lamp on the light pad to move the glints. You can also drag across the sky in the scene.
  3. Stack layers 1 to 6. Each step swaps in a different real shader run.
  4. Compare the nanopillar eye with a bare dome and a flat slab.

The science

Open a drawer to read
Why a moth eye?

A night-flying moth’s compound eye is tiled with facets, and each facet is covered in bumps about 200 nm apart, smaller than the wavelength of light. Bernhard, Miller & Møller (Acta Physiologica Scandinavica, 1965) identified these “corneal nipples” as an antireflection layer, which may stop the eye glinting at predators. Clapham & Hutley (Nature, 1973) made artificial versions to cut reflection from lenses. The same trick now coats camera lenses and solar cells.

The bumps work because they are sub-wavelength. To arriving light they look like a smooth ramp in refractive index from air into the cornea, not like separate surfaces. That is a graded-index effect.

Those predators include bats and owls, so the scene puts a bat on watch. The numbers in the drawings are published ones. The tobacco hawkmoth Manduca sexta has about 27,000 facets per eye, each about 30 µm across (White and colleagues, Journal of Experimental Biology, 2003), and each ommatidium holds nine photoreceptors. Across butterflies the nipples sit 180–240 nm apart and stand up to about 230 nm tall (Stavenga, Foletti, Palasantzas & Arikawa, Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 2006); in a crepuscular moth they stand about 256 nm tall (Yang and colleagues, Frontiers in Physiology, 2024).

What the quantum engine did

Atlas entanglement-shader-v1 implements the quantum collision model of Ferreira, Topel, Fromholz & Wootton (arXiv:2606.29989, 2026). Light rays and the surface mode of each conducting layer are qubits, and every ray–layer collision is a small multi-qubit gate. Running the circuit on Atlas’s classical statevector simulator gives a reflectance R and a transmittance T for every incidence angle and interlayer phase. The engine bakes them into 60×60 lookup tables and returns them with a GLSL shader that reads them. This page runs that shader, ported line by line to WebGL2. In the scene it runs inside the loupe.

Layers and rays share a 21-qubit budget, and the engine only checks it once a job starts, so we had to probe with real jobs. Seven layers allows at most 5 rays, but the engine needs at least as many rays as layers, so 6 layers × 6 rays is the largest stack that fits. A probe with 7 rays at 6 layers was rejected (“at most 6”). Each ray is one qubit, so the 6 × 6 run sits exactly at the 21-qubit limit. Every probe was a real job, and the table below lists them all.

The sweep then holds the rays at 6 and steps the layers from 1 to 6, so only the layer count changes. Everything else stays fixed: single-layer reflectance 0.2, absorption 0.95, and the defaults (interaction 1, style “peaked”, resolution 60). Two control runs switch the nonlinear interaction off (interaction 0) at 1 and 6 layers.

How it was made

The meter, the scene, the film
How the meter works

After each change, the page renders every geometry once more, off-screen, from your exact viewpoint. For each pixel it records the luminance of the engine’s reflectance at that pixel’s viewing angle, then averages over the pixels the object covers. Under a uniform sky, that average is the fraction of light the object sends back to you. Flash is the same average, counted only where a pixel’s mirror direction points within about 3° of the lamp, so it is the share of the surface that mirrors the lamp straight at you. The tilt chart is this meter measured at fixed angles for every run.

Everything in the meter is classical arithmetic on the downloaded lookup tables. The night sky, lamp colour and tone mapping in “Night scene” are our own rendering choices. “Raw R” shows the engine shader’s reflectance output with no scene lighting.

In the scene you watch from the bat’s place, so what the object sends back to you is what the bat sees: the big number under the scene, with one bar per geometry. The tilt chart is in The data, above.

How the bat and the scene work

What in the scene is data: the engraved bat’s eyelid opens in eight steps with that number (fully shut at 22% or less, fully open at 72% or more). The words “Hidden” (under 32%), “Glimpsed” (32–50%) and “Spotted” (50% or more) are our own bands. The flying bat sits on an arc at your viewing tilt: level with the loupe is head-on, overhead is grazing; it swoops when the meter crosses into “Spotted”. The bulb sits where the lamp pad points. The moth’s eye glints when the flash is above 0.01%. In the layer stack, the ten dots are the run’s mean R and T, rounded to tenths. Everything else (the bat’s wingbeat, the stars, the marching lamp rays) is decoration.

The engraving itself is classical drawing, generated from geometry we wrote down from published descriptions and measurements, not from photos: a hawkmoth (Sphingidae) head in profile with its scaled head capsule, compound eye, upcurved labial palp, coiled proboscis, scape and annulated antenna; the facet lenses as 30 µm hexagons; and a big brown bat (Eptesicus fuscus), whose short round-tipped ears, rounded tragus and bare, broad snout follow published descriptions of the species, calling through its open mouth, as this species does. Sources are in the entry’s CREDITS.md.

The offline film and how to rebuild everything

The brief’s deliverable is an MP4 of the shader on a 3D asset. moth_eye.mp4 (the player above) is rendered offline by this same WebGL code in headless Chrome, frame by frame from a fixed script, and piped into ffmpeg. The copy on this page is re-encoded smaller (H.264) to keep the page light. reflectance_vs_layers.png is the plot. Scripts, job IDs and the full parameter table are in the entry’s README and PARAMS.md.

The engraved plates (the moth head, facet inset, flying bat, lamp, the bat-head meter and Plates I and II) are drawn by make_plates.py into web/data/plates.json; make_sprites.py rasterises the same head into the hub mascot.

What this does not claim

Honesty notes

This is an analogy. Real moth eyes suppress reflection with a sub-wavelength graded-index structure. The engine models a stack of thin conducting sheets with quantum-optical interactions, and a shader applied facet by facet is geometric optics, which breaks down at the scale of real nanopillars. Our facet is only 24 pillar pitches across, so you can see the pillars. All circuits ran on Atlas’s classical statevector simulator, not on quantum hardware. We claim no quantum advantage, and the engine’s own “no classical shader can replicate” wording is not a claim we test. Some lookup-table values exceed 1, which the engine documents as HDR output, so we show them unclipped.

Bats hunt mostly by echolocation, not by sight; the bat here is a stand-in for any watcher. The engravings are our own drawings from published descriptions: the facet mesh on the head is about 5× coarser than real and the depth of Plate I is compressed, both so they read at screen size.
