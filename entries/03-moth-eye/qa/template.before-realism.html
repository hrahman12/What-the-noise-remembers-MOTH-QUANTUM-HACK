Why a moth eye?

A night-flying moth’s compound eye is tiled with facets, and each facet is covered in bumps about 200 nm apart, smaller than the wavelength of light. Bernhard, Miller & Møller (Acta Physiologica Scandinavica, 1965) identified these “corneal nipples” as an antireflection layer, which may stop the eye glinting at predators such as bats and owls. Clapham & Hutley (Nature, 1973) made artificial versions to cut reflection from lenses. The same trick now coats camera lenses and solar cells.

The bumps work because they are sub-wavelength. To arriving light they look like a smooth ramp in refractive index from air into the cornea, not like separate surfaces. That is a graded-index effect.

What the quantum engine did

Atlas entanglement-shader-v1 implements the quantum collision model of Ferreira, Topel, Fromholz & Wootton (arXiv:2606.29989, 2026). Light rays and the surface mode of each conducting layer are qubits, and every ray–layer collision is a small multi-qubit gate. Running the circuit on Atlas’s classical statevector simulator gives a reflectance R and a transmittance T for every incidence angle and interlayer phase. The engine bakes them into 60×60 lookup tables and returns them with a GLSL shader that reads them. The loupe runs that shader, ported line by line to WebGL2.

Layers and rays share a 21-qubit budget, and the engine only checks it once a job starts, so we had to probe with real jobs. Seven layers allows at most 5 rays, but the engine needs at least as many rays as layers, so 6 layers × 6 rays is the largest stack that fits. A probe with 7 rays at 6 layers was rejected (“at most 6”). Each ray is one qubit, so the 6 × 6 run sits exactly at the 21-qubit limit. The sweep then holds the rays at 6 and steps the layers from 1 to 6, so only the layer count changes. Everything else stays fixed: single-layer reflectance 0.2, absorption 0.95, and the defaults (interaction 1, style “peaked”, resolution 60). Two control runs switch the nonlinear interaction off (interaction 0) at 1 and 6 layers.

How the bat meter and the scene work

After each change, the page renders every geometry once more, off-screen, from your exact viewpoint. For each pixel it records the luminance of the engine’s reflectance at that pixel’s viewing angle, then averages over the pixels the object covers. Under a uniform sky, that average is the fraction of light the object sends back to the bat, and it is the big number under the scene. Flash is the same average, counted only where a pixel’s mirror direction points within about 3° of the lamp, so it is the share of the surface that mirrors the lamp straight at the bat. The tilt chart in the Data view is this meter measured at fixed angles for every run.

What in the scene is data: the bat’s eyelids open in eight steps with that number (fully shut at 22% or less, fully open at 72% or more). The words “dozing” (under 32%), “peering” (32–50%) and “spotted you” (50% or more) are our own bands, and the moth tucks its wings when the bat is spotting it. The bat sits on an arc at your viewing tilt: level with the loupe is head-on, overhead is grazing. The bulb sits where the lamp pad points. The moth’s eye glints when the flash is above 0.01%. In the layer stack, the ten dots are the run’s mean R and T, rounded to tenths. Everything else (wing flaps, stars, the little moths at the bulb) is decoration.

Everything in the meter is classical arithmetic on the downloaded lookup tables. The night sky, lamp colour and tone mapping in “Night scene” are our own rendering choices. “Raw R” shows the engine shader’s reflectance output with no scene lighting.

What this does not claim

This is an analogy. Real moth eyes suppress reflection with a sub-wavelength graded-index structure. The engine models a stack of thin conducting sheets with quantum-optical interactions, and a shader applied facet by facet is geometric optics, which breaks down at the scale of real nanopillars. Our facet is only 24 pillar pitches across, so you can see the pillars. All circuits ran on Atlas’s classical statevector simulator, not on quantum hardware. We claim no quantum advantage, and the engine’s own “no classical shader can replicate” wording is not a claim we test. Some lookup-table values exceed 1, which the engine documents as HDR output, so we show them unclipped. Bats hunt mostly by echolocation, not by sight; the bat here is a stand-in for any watcher.

The offline film and how to rebuild everything

The brief’s deliverable is an MP4 of the shader on a 3D asset. moth_eye.mp4 (the player above) is rendered offline by this same WebGL code in headless Chrome, frame by frame from a fixed script, and piped into ffmpeg. The copy on this page is re-encoded smaller (H.264) to keep the page light. reflectance_vs_layers.png is the plot. The pixel cast is drawn by make_sprites.py into web/sprites.json. Scripts, job IDs and the full parameter table are in the entry’s README and PARAMS.md.
