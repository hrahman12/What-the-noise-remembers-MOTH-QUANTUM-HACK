How to play

You are in control
  1. Pick real codas, the reservoir, or the handoff between them. Or tap a whale in the sea.
  2. Choose a whale for real codas, or a variation level and take for the reservoir. Each reservoir take is its own engine job.
  3. Play, pause and scrub. Clicks light up on the timeline as they sound. In the sea, every click lights its whale's head and sends out a ring; the timeline and token histogram are in The data, just below.

Keys. Space plays or pauses, arrow keys skip 5 seconds. On the sea: keys 1 to 4 pick a whale, A all whales, R the reservoir, H the submitted track; a tap on open water plays or pauses. Copy link to this moment saves the track and the second you are on.

How it was made

Open a drawer
What the quantum reservoir did

Training (qrc-train-v2, 1 job). The whole corpus, in recording order, went in as one 3,840-token sequence. The reservoir is 12 qubits, the engine's maximum (13 is rejected by its schema). Each token drives one reservoir step with 10 random gates mixed in; mixing 0.7 sets how much each step replaces the reservoir's memory, and each step is measured with 3,000 shots. A readout from the measurements to the 30 tokens (the returned model holds a 4,096 × 30 weight matrix; 4,096 = 212, the number of 12-qubit outcomes) is trained for 100 epochs to predict the next token. Training windows are 16 tokens long (the first 4 are washout), and 12.5% of the candidate windows were sampled. Parameter meanings are from the engine's own schema.

Generation (qrc-gen-v2, one job per setting). Each job loads the same trained model by reference (the training job's state output, passed by its asset ID, never chained from another generation), warms the reservoir up, then samples tokens one at a time. Variation is the sampling temperature: low repeats the likeliest token, high spreads toward every token. Warm-up is the whole vocabulary (the engine default) or, for the handoff, the 31 real codas you hear first. Levels 0.25 to 4 have two takes (random_seed 1 and 2); 16 and 64 have one. Every take is its own job.

What we found. With the seed fixed, the variation setting changes far less than you might expect. Levels 0.25 and 0.5 gave identical sequences for both seeds; 1 departs from them only after 155 codas (seed 1) or 93 (seed 2); 2 and 4 share their first 64 codas for seed 1 and are identical for seed 2. The seed mattered more than the temperature in this range. That is why we added 16 and 64, where the readout's preferences finally flatten: at 64 all 30 tokens appear and only 11% of codas repeat the previous type. Every sequence from the default warm-up opens the same way (25, then 16 or 25).

Every job ran on Atlas's server-side Qiskit Aer simulator. These engines have no hardware mode.

How the sound is made (classical)

No recordings are used. Every click is the same synthetic broadband pulse, deliberately kept low (half its energy lies below about 2.7 kHz) so it carries on small speakers. That is the "pitched down" step. It changes only the click's colour: click timing is never stretched at 1×.

Real codas play their recorded inter-click intervals at the recorded times. Silences longer than 2.5 s are shortened to 2.5 s, and each caller label is panned to its own place in stereo. Reservoir codas have only a token, so each is played from that token's prototype: the mean normalised rhythm times the token's median length. The silence before each generated coda is drawn, classically, from the real silences (0.3 to 2.5 s). The reservoir has no whale identity, so it sits in the centre.

The ½× button halves the playback speed for listening. It stretches timing and is labelled as such.

The science

Codas as rhythm tokens

Why sperm-whale codas?

Sperm whales exchange codas: short, patterned bursts of clicks. The data here are 3,840 codas from the Dominica Sperm Whale Project, published with Sharma et al., Contextual and combinatorial structure in sperm whale vocalisations, Nature Communications 15, 3617 (2024). Each row gives a coda's number of clicks, the gaps between its clicks (inter-click intervals), which caller made it, and when. Callers are labelled 1, 2, 3 and so on within each recording, so "whale 2" is a label, not one animal across the corpus.

We turn every coda into a token: its click count plus a rhythm letter. The letter is our own k-means cluster of the coda's inter-click intervals divided by its total length, fitted per click count. That gives 30 tokens. The 5-click codas split cleanly into a slow pattern (two long gaps, then three short ones: 5A) and a fast even one (5B). These are not the paper's rhythm types: they are a simple, reproducible stand-in.

What this does not claim

Honesty

No meaning is claimed; codas are treated as rhythm tokens. Nothing here translates, decodes or speaks whale. The reservoir learned which token tends to follow which; it knows nothing about why. The reservoir ran on a classical simulator of a quantum circuit, not quantum hardware, and we claim no quantum advantage. Tokenisation, timing, click synthesis, stereo placement and every statistic are classical.
