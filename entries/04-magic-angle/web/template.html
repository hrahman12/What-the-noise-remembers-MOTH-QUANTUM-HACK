The scienceOpen a drawer
Why twist graphene?

Lay one honeycomb on another and turn it a little. Where the atoms line up (AA) and where they interleave (AB) alternate on a much larger lattice: a moiré superlattice with period L = a / (2 sin θ/2). The smaller the twist, the larger the pattern. The “Original” envelope view shows it.

Bistritzer and MacDonald (PNAS 108, 12233, 2011) predicted that at certain “magic” twists the electrons' lowest moiré band goes flat. Cao, Fatemi, Fang, Watanabe, Taniguchi, Kaxiras and Jarillo-Herrero (Nature 556, 43, 2018) found superconductivity in twisted bilayer graphene near 1.1°. With graphene's a = 0.246 nm, the period there is about 12.8 nm.

Nothing about the geometry is special at 1.1°: the pattern simply keeps growing as θ shrinks. The magic is in the electrons, and this piece does not simulate them.

How it was madeOpen a drawer
What the quantum engine did

Atlas telablur-v1 loads two images into one quantum state: 20 pixel qubits address a 1024 × 1024 frame (220 amplitudes), and one selector qubit says which image. Here image1 is layer A and image2 is layer B. Rotating the selector mixes the two layers at the amplitude level. That makes 21 qubits per job, worked out from the engine's documented rule; the engine reports only the image. Each frame took 11–30 s on Atlas's statevector simulator.

The pixel qubits also get a blur rotation; the engine's direction parameter picks which ones. In our probes the scramble grew with strength, and we measured mirror-image copies of each layer. That is what a Gray-coded register does (as in Atlas blur-v1): each pixel is mixed with mirror partners within blocks. At strength 0.25 the output looks like speckle. The lattice survives only in the Fourier spectrum.

Five probe jobs at 1.10° chose the setting. At 0.5 a 4-pixel register pattern dominates. At 0.1 the honeycomb is visible, but layer B carries only about 2 % of layer A's Bragg power. At 0.25 it carries about a third. The probes also caught a trap: with a symmetric twist, layer B is an exact mirror image of layer A, so the register's mirror copies looked like a morph. Rotating the whole bilayer by 15° moves the mirror copies 30° away in Fourier space, so a peak at 15° + θ/2 can only come from image2.

One more fingerprint: the morph frames show extra power exactly on the 15° bisector. That matches the sum-frequency term GA + GB that appears when amplitudes are added before squaring, (√A + √B)², and not when images are simply averaged. It is consistent with the documented amplitude-level mixing; we did not prove it is the only cause.

How the period is measured (classical)

Each 1024 × 1024 morph is Hann-windowed and Fourier transformed (zero-padded to 4096). For the 18 Bragg vectors of the input lattice below 0.45 cycles/px, the power is read along an arc of radius |G| from 10° to 20°, then averaged within and across shells (the left card). The bilayer is built symmetric about 15°, so the rule scores every split 2δ by the profile at −δ times the profile at +δ, and keeps the best. Splits narrower than the untwisted frame's own peak width (° each side) are not searched. A best pair stuck on that limit is unresolved. The ring radius gives the lattice constant am, and the period is Lm = am / (2 sin θm/2), the same as 4π / (√3 |GB − GA|). Nothing in the rule reads the frame's angle.

The register's echo. The 0° frame has no twist at all, yet the rule still finds a symmetric pair ° apart there. That pair is made by the qubit register. By coincidence it sits almost exactly at the magic angle. Any frame whose split matches it is flagged null, because it can't be told apart from that echo. That covers the frames at °. Across 1.05–1.40° the split hardly moves (slope ), while from 1.5° to 5° it follows the twist (slope , ideal 1).

Result. From 1.5° to 5°, all frames give a period that tracks the twist. The median ratio to a / (2 sin θ/2) is (range –). The read-out runs a little short between 1.6° and 2.5°. The 1.00° frame passes the rule but reads 34 % high. Below that, every frame is either the echo or unresolved, so of frames count in all. The method check runs a plain two-peak fit on the original layers, which the morph never touched, and agrees to within  % from 0.4° up. The first rule tried on the morph (a plain two-peak fit) was right to within 10 % in only frames. It is kept in out/measure.json for the record.

The envelope lens is classical post-processing. The three first-shell Bragg directions are band-passed in a fixed band around 15°, and their envelopes are summed. Deep ink means the two layers are in phase there (AA stacking). The same band is used for every frame and for both sources.

What this does not claimHonesty

Geometry only. No band structure, electrons or superconductivity are simulated, and nothing here explains why 1.1° is magic. Every morph frame is a real telablur-v1 job on Atlas's classical statevector simulator, not quantum hardware. The quantum step doesn't compute the moiré and has no advantage: it scrambles the bilayer, and classical Fourier analysis recovers what survives. Near the magic angle it recovers the register's echo, not the twist. The magic angle zone is marked, not resolved. The measurement rule was revised once after the first rule failed on the morph. Both rules' results are published. The Original raw view is drawn in your browser from the same formula as lattice.py. Raw views are the centre 384 × 384 px of each 1024 px frame at 1:1. The record shows the frame's central disc. The slider snaps to the 45 computed angles. So does the turntable rim. Lec, Ay, Bee and Echo are cartoons whose moods follow the published readings; Lec's AA spots come from a / (2 sin θ/2) geometry, scaled to the period that was read.

Every job on this page

Every morph here is one telablur-v1 job on Atlas's classical statevector simulator, 1 credit and 21 qubits each: 45 sweep frames from 0° to 5° at strength 0.25, plus 5 probe jobs at 1.10° that chose the setting (their table is under How it was made). The chosen probe is also the sweep's 1.10° frame, so the page shows 49 unique jobs. No job failed, and none ran on quantum hardware.
