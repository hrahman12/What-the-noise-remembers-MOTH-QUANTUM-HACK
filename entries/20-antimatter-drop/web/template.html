The science

Open a drawer
What antimatter is

Every kind of particle has an antiparticle with the same mass and the opposite electric charge. Paul Dirac's 1928 theory of the electron led to the prediction of a positively charged twin, and Carl Anderson found that positron in cosmic rays in 1932.

Antihydrogen is the simplest anti-atom: one antiproton orbited by one positron. It is electrically neutral, which matters here: charged antiparticles are pushed around by stray fields far stronger than gravity. The ALPHA-g paper puts it starkly: gravity on a proton is equivalent to an electric field of only about 10-7 V/m.

When antimatter touches matter, the two annihilate. An antiproton meeting the trap wall turns into a spray of charged pions, and ALPHA-g's time projection chamber tracks those pions back to the spot where the atom died. Each flash in the trap above is one of those annihilations.

Antihydrogen can only be held in a vacuum, in a magnetic bottle that grips its tiny magnetic moment. ALPHA-g's bottle is a vertical cylinder 4.4 cm across and 25.6 cm tall, deep enough to hold atoms with energies up to about 0.5 K.

Why care which way it falls? General relativity's weak equivalence principle says everything falls the same way, whatever it is made of. Antimatter had never been dropped directly, and some cosmological models assumed matter and antimatter repel.

How ALPHA-g weighed antimatter

About 100 atoms were stacked over 50 production cycles (roughly four hours). A first step released about half of them to count the sample. Then the two mirror coils that cap the trap, G on top and A at the bottom, were ramped down together over 20 seconds. The atoms leaked out over the lower barrier and hit the walls above or below the trap.

A small extra current in coil G set a magnetic bias: a field difference between the two barrier peaks, quoted in units of g. A positive bias makes the top barrier higher and pushes atoms out of the bottom. One g of bias is a field difference of just 4.53 × 10-4 T between peaks of about 1.74 T. In a naive 1-D picture, a bias of −1 g exactly cancels ordinary gravity.

The team ran 11 biases from −3 g to +3 g, six or seven trials each, interleaved over about 30 days. They counted escapes between 10 and 20 s of the ramp in fixed up and down regions. Calibration runs at ±10 g sent atoms almost entirely one way.

Hydrogen released from a symmetric trap would leave through the bottom about 80% of the time, according to their simulations. Antihydrogen's escape curve crossed 50/50 near −1 g. Comparing it to 3-D trajectory simulations gave an acceleration towards Earth of a = (0.75 ± 0.13 (statistical + systematic) ± 0.16 (simulation)) g, consistent with 1 g. The chance of a result this extreme with no gravity is 2.9 × 10-4. Repulsive gravity of 1 g fits with a probability below 10-15, so it is ruled out.

What the quantum computer did

Atlas's comet-qrng-v1 engine put %%FEZ_REG%% qubits of IBM's %%FEZ_BACKEND%% into an equal superposition and measured them 10,000 times. It added 8 more qubits as four Bell pairs (CHSH witness S = %%FEZ_S%%, above the classical limit of 2). That is %%FEZ_QUBITS%% qubits, the whole chip. The engine estimated how much unpredictability the raw bits really carried, using NIST SP 800-90B estimators (%%FEZ_HBIT%% bits of min-entropy per raw bit). It then compressed them with a Toeplitz extractor set to ε = 2-128, leaving %%FEZ_BYTES%% bytes. Job %%FEZ_JOB%%, IBM job %%FEZ_PJOB%%.

Each escaping atom reads the next 16 bits of that bank as a number k from 0 to 65,535. If k is below round(Pdn × 65,536), the atom goes down; otherwise it goes up. Pdn comes from Table 1 for the bias you set. The dice log under the verdict shows every comparison. It keeps the last eight. The die beside the trap rolls once per escaping atom and shows that word and its verdict. Press Hear the dice to hear every roll as it lands.

The emulator bank is the same engine on a classical Aer simulator: %%EMU_QUBITS%% qubits (the emulator's cap), %%EMU_BYTES%% bytes, job %%EMU_JOB%%. The browser dice come from your browser's crypto random generator. Switch between all three mid-ramp: the curve converges to the same place, because the physics lives in the probabilities, not the dice.

What this does not claim

Honesty notes

This page does not simulate antihydrogen. Each atom's up or down outcome is a weighted coin, and the weights are Table 1's background-corrected counts. Those counts are not corrected for detector efficiency (the ±10 g calibration rows suggest the up and down regions differ by a few per cent), and the bias labels are the nominal ones. The paper's own escape curve applies both corrections and puts the balance point close to −1 g; our logistic fit to the raw counts gives −0.78 g.

Everything else here is classical and illustrative: when each atom escapes (spread evenly over 10 to 20 s of the ramp, the window where the paper counts its events), the motion in the trap, the vertex positions, the pion tracks and the |B| sketch. The balance-point fit and the "other worlds" curves are our own logistic cartoon, not the paper's simulation. The real 0.75 g comes from 3-D trajectory simulations we do not reproduce.

The sprite scene is illustration too: the detector pads lighting up and the barrier "gates" at the mirror coils. The drawing keeps heights to scale but widens the trap and the atoms so you can see them, and the bias on the gates is exaggerated. The characters' faces only react: an atom looks down or up after its die has decided, and the mascot stands wherever your own fit puts it. The signposts on its ruler belong to the same logistic cartoon. The optional "Hear the dice" sound is a classical sonification of the same rolls: one short tone per die, high for up and low for down; in a fast campaign some tones are skipped so they never pile up.

The quantum computer contributes nothing to the physics: it only supplies dice. The %%FEZ_BACKEND%% bytes are entropy-accounted under the engine's stated assumption that qubits within a shot carry no more than pairwise correlations (grade "%%FEZ_GRADE%%"). That is not device-independent certification: the CHSH value is a fidelity witness. The emulator bank is classical pseudo-randomness.
