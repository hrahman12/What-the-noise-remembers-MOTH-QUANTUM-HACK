Chapter 1 · London, 1861

Three grey photos make one colour photo.

In 1861 James Clerk Maxwell wanted to prove an idea: every colour we see can be built from just three amounts, of red, green and blue light.

The photographer Thomas Sutton took three black-and-white pictures of a tartan ribbon, each through a different coloured filter. On its own, each plate is only grey. It records how much of one colour got through, and nothing else.

Then they shone the three pictures through the same three filters onto one screen, on top of each other. The ribbon came back in colour.

Try it: switch the filters on and off. With one filter you get a grey plate. With all three you get the colour back. You can also click the picture to step through the plates one at a time.

Classical: your browser splits a modern scan of the photo into three channels. That's what the filters did, but digitally. Nothing in this chapter is quantum.

The whole 1861 photograph. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons (File:Tartan Ribbon.jpg).

Chapter 2 · The thing you can't look at

A qubit is an arrow you can only ask yes-or-no questions.

Picture a qubit as an arrow inside a ball. You never get to see the arrow. When you measure it, you get a single click: 0 or 1. Then it's gone, and you need a fresh copy to ask again.

If you measure along the up–down direction (called Z), an arrow pointing straight up always clicks 0. An arrow on the equator clicks 0 and 1 equally, like a fair coin. So many clicks tell you how far up the arrow points.

But Z clicks say nothing about which way the arrow leans around the equator. For that you have to ask along two other directions, X and Y. That gives three directions with lots of shots each, and from them you rebuild the arrow. Physicists call this quantum state tomography, and it's Maxwell's trick again: three filtered measurements, one picture.

This game is a classical toy. Its clicks come from your browser's random-number generator, using the odds a real qubit would have. It shows the method. The real quantum runs are in the ball at the top. Maxwell's studio at the top shows the same runs.

Chapter 3 · Colours live in a ball too

Grey in the middle, white on top, black underneath.

Put every colour into a ball: white at the north pole, black at the south pole, and 50% grey right in the centre. The bright, strong colours sit around the equator: red, then yellow, green, cyan, blue and magenta all the way round. Pale colours are near the top, dark ones near the bottom, and dull greyish ones close to the middle.

That ball has the same shape as the qubit ball. Up–down plays the part of Z, and the direction around the equator (the hue) plays the part of the X–Y lean. So every colour is an arrow with three numbers, X, Y and Z, just like a qubit's state. Atlas's Tessa engine is built on that match, and so is this page.

Analogy, labelledA colour isn't a qubit and photography isn't quantum. The ball is a map: it lets a program store a colour as the angles of a qubit, then measure it back.

A neat bonus: the very centre of the qubit ball is the most uncertain state possible, a perfect 50/50 coin in every direction. On the colour ball that spot is grey.

Where your colour sits

Its X, Y and Z are the three plates on Maxwell's line at the top. A white plate means +1, black means −1, mid grey means 0.

Chapter 4 · What the quantum engine did

Write the colour onto qubits, measure, rebuild.

The plan was to hand the ribbon to the Atlas engine tessa-image-v1, which writes pictures onto qubits through a colour ball like this one. On hack night it timed out five times in a row. So we did its job by hand with its sibling engine qpixl-v1, which uses the same encoding library.

Each colour becomes its arrow's three numbers: how far it points along X, along Y and along Z. Those are exactly the three numbers tomography is after. We sent every X number, then every Y, then every Z, like three plates. qpixl stored each number as the angle of a qubit, measured it again and again (the shots), and gave the number back. Our code then put each colour back together from its three numbers.

We sent 112 test colours spread through the ball, plus the ribbon, as large as each machine had room for. We ran it on three kinds of machine: a perfect simulator (no noise at all), simulators that copy the noise of two real IBM chips, and once on a real IBM quantum chip.

Click a cell to load that run into the ball. Each number is how far the 112 test colours landed from where they started, on average, in ball units where the radius is 1 (0 is perfect). Maxwell's studio at the top loads the same run.

Chapter 5 · The ribbon, again

Maxwell's ribbon, rebuilt by measurement.

The same runs also rebuilt the ribbon itself. Drag the divider to compare the picture we sent in (left) with what came back (right), for whichever machine and shots you picked above. The perfect simulator had room for a 35×35 ribbon. The Fez chip and its model hold only 448 numbers, so they got a 6×6 one, and the Brisbane model had no room for the ribbon at all.

Click any pixel to send that exact pixel's colour to the ball. The rebuild you see there comes from that pixel's own three measured numbers. The arrow keys move the pixel too, and it also becomes the sitter in Maxwell's studio at the top.

Words

Glossary.

Qubit
The quantum version of a bit. Its state is an arrow in a ball, not just 0 or 1.
Measurement
Asking a qubit one yes-or-no question along one direction. You get a single 0 or 1, and the original state is gone.
Shot
One run of the circuit ending in one measurement. Many shots turn random clicks into reliable odds.
Basis (X, Y, Z)
The direction you measure along. Z is up–down; X and Y are two directions around the equator.
Bloch sphere
The ball that every single-qubit state lives in. Pure states sit on the surface; noisy, uncertain states sit inside.
Tomography
Rebuilding a state you can't see from measurements along several directions. The name comes from medical scans, which rebuild a body from many slices.
Noise
Tiny unwanted disturbances in a real chip. They blur the arrow and pull it toward the centre of the ball.
Simulator
An ordinary computer working out what a perfect quantum computer would do. Here that's Atlas's aer.
Noise model
A simulator that adds the measured noise of a real chip: fake_fez copies IBM's Fez chip, fake_brisbane copies Brisbane.
Hardware
A real quantum chip. IBM's ibm_fez has 156 qubits, cooled close to absolute zero.
Three-colour photography
Maxwell's method: three filtered grey photos recombined into colour. Classical optics, no quantum physics.

Why Maxwell's ribbon?

Maxwell proposed three-colour photography in 1855 and showed this ribbon at the Royal Institution in London in May 1861, with photographs taken by Thomas Sutton. It's considered the first durable colour photograph.

His point was that colour is a three-number quantity, and you can measure it one filtered number at a time. A qubit's state is also a three-number quantity, the three components of its arrow, and you can also only measure it one direction at a time. The analogy stops there. Light through a filter is classical optics.
