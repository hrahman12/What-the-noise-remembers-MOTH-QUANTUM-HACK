Why Lenia?

Lenia (Bert Wang-Chak Chan, Complex Systems 28(3), 2019) makes the Game of Life continuous in space, time and state. Each step, every cell sums its neighbourhood through a smooth ring-shaped kernel, and a bell-shaped growth curve turns that sum into growth or decay. Hundreds of creature-like patterns live in it. Orbium unicaudatus is the classic glider.

The kernel is the creature's sense of touch: it decides how far, and how strongly, each cell feels the others. Change only the kernel and the same creature lives a different life.

How it was madeblur-core-v1 · 18 + 20 qubits
What the quantum engine did

Engine: Atlas blur-core-v1, run on Atlas's classical statevector simulator (exact probabilities, no shots). It amplitude-encodes a grid onto a Gray-coded qubit register, applies Rx rotations to the register's qubits (strength sets the angle, reach sets how far up the register they go), and reads the probabilities back out, rescaled to the input's maximum. Neighbouring cells differ by one bit, so the rotations spread each value onto its neighbours: a blur made of interference.

The engine's own job status reports the register: "Recovered 18-qubit grid from measurement" for the kernels and "Recovered 20-qubit grid" for the snapshot.

Kernels: four kernel shells (ring, Gaussian, polynomial and step), each on the full 256 × 256 grid the browser convolves with, stacked into one [4, 256, 256] grid. That is 2 + 8 + 8 = 18 qubits, with axes [1, 2] so the four never mix. Three jobs: strength 0.25, 0.5 and 1.0, reach 0, style x.

Snapshot: a 528 × 528 classical Lenia world. 528 is over 512 per side, so the engine pads each axis to 1024: 10 + 10 = 20 qubits (about 27% of the register holds the world, the rest is padding). Two jobs: strength 0.5 and 0.25, reach 0.

Why not 24 qubits: the Atlas API rejects request bodies over 1 MiB (HTTP 413, measured with free invalid-parameter probes), and a 1024 × 1024 grid is 2.1 MB even as zeros. Results are capped too. Three of our eight paid jobs failed after the simulation finished with a result-payload error (TMPRL1103): reach 1 on the kernels (twice), because a fully non-local blur makes every one of 262,144 outputs a non-zero float, and a first snapshot with creatures spread over the whole world. The colony now sits in the top-left 128 × 128 corner so the output stays small.

Measured locality: at reach 0 the outputs are exactly zero outside aligned Gray-code blocks: 16 cells wide on the 256-cell kernel axes (so each kernel stays inside a 32 × 32 window) and 64 to 128 cells wide on the 1024-cell snapshot axes. So reach 0 rotates only the lower qubits of each axis, not the whole register.

About the characters

Orbi, the eyes, the pipette, the brush, the eraser and the lenses are pixel art in the shared ink palette (web/sprites.json). They never invent data. Eyes sit on every live blob that counts as Orbium-sized by the same rule as the counter (mass 55 to 95, as in analyze.py), and look the way that blob actually moves. A ghost rises when a blob that had counted for at least 30 steps stops counting (or when you erase or overpaint one). A sparkle marks one you dropped, or a new one once it has counted for 30 steps. The 30-step wait only keeps flickering soup from spawning ghosts; the counter itself uses no wait. Their blinking is decoration.

The fate strip under the lenses replays the measured result for the chosen kernel and shell: Orbi glides at the measured speed (0.61 cells per step becomes a fixed on-screen pace), the pale outline keeps the original kernel's pace, and the break-up and flood scenes stand for the measured fates, not for their timing. Orbi in the corner pulls a dizzy face when the live dish floods (mass over 4,000).

What this does not claimhonesty

The dynamics are classical Lenia, computed in your browser (and in Python for the film). Only the kernels and the snapshots went through the quantum blur, and that blur ran on a classical statevector simulator, not quantum hardware. This is not a quantum cellular automaton, shows no quantum advantage, and "Quantum Lenia" is just the name of this pipeline.

The fates shown in the kernel panel were measured in our own runs (one Orbium, 2,000 steps, μ 0.15, σ 0.015). Other seeds or settings can behave differently. Snapshot values are shown and seeded at 16-bit precision. The Orbium pattern is from Bert Chan's MIT-licensed Lenia repository.
